Most people commission a trade mark search hoping for a yes or a no. What a search
actually produces is neither: it produces a picture of the space a proposed mark
would have to occupy, and an assessment of how crowded that space already is.

That distinction matters, because it changes what you should do with the result.

## What a search covers

A search of the register shows marks that have been applied for or registered in
the relevant classes, along with their status, their owners and their dates. It
will surface identical marks immediately. Its real value, though, is in the
near-misses — marks that are not the same as yours but are close enough that an
examiner or a proprietor might say they are.

A search of use in the market is a separate exercise. Unregistered marks can still
be asserted against you if the other party has been using them, and the register
will not show them.

## What a search cannot do

It cannot bind the Registry. Two examiners can take different views of the same
pair of marks, and the tests applied — deceptive similarity, likelihood of
confusion, distinctiveness — involve judgement rather than arithmetic.

It also cannot see the future. A search reflects the register on the day it is run.
An application filed a week earlier may not yet be visible.

## What to do with the result

A clean search is not permission to stop thinking; a search that turns up conflicts
is not necessarily a reason to abandon a name. The useful output is a ranking:
which of your candidate names is most defensible, in which classes, and what would
need to be true for the weaker ones to work.

The cost of running a search is small. The cost of discovering the problem after
signage, packaging and three years of goodwill is not.

## Before you file

Two practical points. First, get the classes right — a mark registered for the
wrong goods or services protects very little. Second, make sure the applicant is
the entity that will actually own and use the mark. Corrections after the fact are
possible, but they are slower and more expensive than getting it right at filing.
