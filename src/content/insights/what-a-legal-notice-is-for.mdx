A legal notice has a reputation it does not deserve. It is treated either as an
aggressive opening move or as an empty ritual before a case is filed. It is
usually neither.

At its most useful, a notice does three things: it fixes the facts as one party
understands them, it states the obligation being relied on, and it makes a specific
demand within a stated period. All three of those have value regardless of what
happens next.

## It resolves a fair number of disputes

Many disputes persist because the other side has not been confronted with a clear,
unemotional account of the position. A notice that sets out dates, documents and a
precise demand — without rhetoric — frequently produces a response that months of
correspondence did not.

## It becomes part of the record

If proceedings follow, the notice and the reply to it are read. A notice that
overstates the claim, gets a date wrong or demands something the contract does not
support will be used against the party who sent it. A restrained, accurate notice
does the opposite: it makes the eventual pleading easier to write and harder to
answer.

Silence in reply is itself informative, and is frequently pointed to later.

## It clarifies whether the matter is worth pursuing

Drafting a notice forces the exercise that should happen anyway — assembling the
chronology, locating the documents, identifying the exact term relied on. That
process quite often reveals that the claim is stronger than assumed, or weaker.
Either way it is better to know before filing.

## What a notice should not be

It should not exaggerate. It should not demand relief that no forum could grant.
It should not set a deadline the sender has no intention of acting on, because a
deadline that passes without consequence teaches the other side exactly how much
the threat is worth.

## When to skip it

Where the delay would defeat the remedy — where assets are being moved, or an
irreversible step is imminent — a notice may simply warn the other party in time
to act. In those cases urgent relief comes first.

Some categories of matter require a notice as a precondition. Whether yours does
is a question of the specific claim and the party involved, and is worth
establishing before anything is sent.
