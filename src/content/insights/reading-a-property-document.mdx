Property agreements are long, and the length is not accidental. The clauses that
decide what happens when something goes wrong tend to sit well past the point at
which most buyers stop reading closely.

What follows is not a substitute for having a document reviewed. It is a short list
of the places where problems usually originate.

## Who is conveying, and on what basis

Start with the recitals. They should trace how the seller came to hold the property
and refer to the documents that establish it. If the chain in the recitals does not
match the documents annexed, that gap is the first thing to resolve — not the price.

## What exactly is being sold

Area, boundaries, the share in common areas, parking, and whether what is described
matches the sanctioned plan. Descriptions drafted from a brochure rather than from
the approved plan are a recurring source of dispute.

## Possession

The date, the conditions attached to it, and what happens if it is missed. Note
whether the date is expressed as a fixed date or as a period running from an event
that has not yet occurred — the second is far weaker than it looks.

## Payment and its triggers

Payments tied to construction milestones need those milestones defined in a way
that can be verified by someone other than the developer. "On commencement of
finishing work" means less than it appears to.

## Default — on both sides

Read the buyer's default clause and the seller's default clause together. If one
attracts forfeiture and interest while the other attracts a refund without
compensation, that asymmetry is worth negotiating before signature rather than
litigating afterwards.

## Approvals and contingencies

Where the transaction depends on a permission that has not yet been granted, the
agreement should say what happens if it is refused or delayed. Silence on that
point usually operates against the buyer.

## Termination and dispute resolution

Whether the agreement provides for arbitration, which seat and which forum, and
whether that is convenient to you. These clauses are ignored at signature and
become decisive later.

## One general point

Do not pay an advance before the documents have been read. Once money has moved,
the practical leverage to correct a clause largely disappears — and a review that
would have taken a few days becomes a negotiation you are conducting from behind.
